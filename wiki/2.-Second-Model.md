# Position-specific probabilities, rewriting and code distance

We continue [[the first model|1.-First-Model]] with the same corpus:

```text
red fox rests
blue fox sleeps
red hare sleeps
```

We now model an ordered three-word sequence. We estimate a separate distribution for each position and assume independence between positions. This gives $P(\text{red fox rests})=4/27$ and $P(\text{red fox fox})=0$.

## We retain the positions

Let $X_1,X_2,X_3$ be its words. Each corpus column contains three tokens, so the position-specific estimates are:

| Position | Observed counts | Positive probabilities |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1, colour | red: 2; blue: 1 | $P_1(\text{red})=2/3$; $P_1(\text{blue})=1/3$ |
| 2, animal | fox: 2; hare: 1 | $P_2(\text{fox})=2/3$; $P_2(\text{hare})=1/3$ |
| 3, action | rests: 1; sleeps: 2 | $P_3(\text{rests})=1/3$; $P_3(\text{sleeps})=2/3$ |

Every other word has probability zero in the respective position under these unsmoothed observed-frequency estimates. Under our assumption that the three positions are independent, we obtain

```math
P(X_1=w_1,X_2=w_2,X_3=w_3)
=P_1(w_1)P_2(w_2)P_3(w_3).
```

Consequently,

```math
\begin{aligned}
P(\text{red fox rests})
&=\frac23\cdot\frac23\cdot\frac13
=\frac4{27}
\approx0.148148,\\[4pt]
P(\text{red fox fox})
&=\frac23\cdot\frac23\cdot0
=0.
\end{aligned}
```

The second result follows because _fox_ never occupies the third position in our corpus. This zero belongs to the specified model; three observations cannot establish a universal impossibility.

Previously, we chose one of the nine corpus positions uniformly and asked for that token’s identity. For example, its pooled probability of _fox_ was $2/9$. That experiment supplied a one-word distribution and entropy, without assigning probabilities to whole sequences. We now retain the positions and add an explicit rule for combining their distributions. The product distribution has eight possible sequences and total probability

```math
\left(\frac23+\frac13\right)
\left(\frac23+\frac13\right)
\left(\frac13+\frac23\right)=1.
```

Independence does not require identical distributions. Here, the distributions preserve colour–animal–action order, so every positive-probability sequence satisfies our earlier slot grammar. They also permit unobserved recombinations: _blue hare rests_ has probability $(1/3)^3=1/27$. Independence removes dependencies _between the slot choices_; it does not erase the restrictions inside each slot. For a richer grammar, agreement or other dependencies could require a richer probability model. These three sentences do not themselves establish independence.

## We follow an ordered rewrite scheme

For the rewrite scheme, we inspect rules in their listed order at every step, then replace the leftmost occurrence of the selected rule’s left side:

```text
1. bb → Q    ordinary
2. a  → Z    final
```

| Step | Current word | Selected rule | Replacement | Result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | `abb` | 1 | `bb` at characters 2–3 becomes `Q` | `aQ` |
| 2 | `aQ` | 2, final | `a` at character 1 becomes `Z` | `ZQ`, halt |

Although `a` occurs earlier in the initial word, rule 1 has priority. After that ordinary substitution, we search again from rule 1. It no longer applies, so rule 2 applies and stops the procedure immediately after its substitution.

This is a _deterministic Markov normal algorithm_. The rules, their order and the occurrence convention determine the next step. No random transition is being chosen, so this scheme is not a stochastic Markov model.

## We distinguish detection from correction

For the binary code $C=\{0000,1111\}$, we count differing bit positions:

```math
d(0000,1111)=4,
\qquad d_{\min}=4.
```

Unique correction of every error affecting at most $t$ distinct bits requires $d_{\min}>2t$. For two flipped bits we would need $4>4$, which fails. The guaranteed correction radius is

```math
\left\lfloor\frac{d_{\min}-1}{2}\right\rfloor
=\left\lfloor\frac32\right\rfloor
=1.
```

We can see the obstruction directly by receiving `0011`:

| Possible transmitted word | Two flipped positions | Received word | Distance |
| --- | --- | --- | ---: |
| `0000` | 3 and 4 | `0011` | 2 |
| `1111` | 1 and 2 | `0011` | 2 |

The received word is equally close to both codewords. Even knowing that _exactly_ two bits flipped leaves both possibilities. A decoder cannot always identify the transmitted word from this received block alone. We can detect that `0011` is not a codeword, but cannot guarantee correcting it.
